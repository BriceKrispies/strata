Everyone who has taught beginners knows the first hundred hours. They are wonderful. A person who has never centred clay on a wheel learns to do it, roughly, in an afternoon, and by the end of a month can pull a cylinder that stands up. The improvement is visible from one week to the next. People in this stage are a joy to teach and tend to bring cake.

Nobody warns them about the second hundred.

## The plateau has a shape

Somewhere around the fourth month, the curve goes flat. The cylinders stand up but they all look the same. The student can now *see* what a good pot looks like, which they could not before, and what they see is that theirs are not good. Their eye has overtaken their hands. Every session produces six objects that are plainly, measurably better than anything they made in the spring, and they go home discouraged for the first time.

This is where most people stop. Not at the beginning, when it is hard, but here, when it has become possible to tell how far there is to go.

I used to think of this as a motivation problem and tried to solve it with encouragement. That does not work, because the student is right. Their pots are not good yet. Telling them otherwise only teaches them that I cannot see either.

## What is happening underneath

What I say now is closer to the truth, which is that the plateau is not a pause in learning. It is a change in what is being learned.

In the first hundred hours you acquire *moves*: centre, open, pull, collar. Each is a new thing your hands can do, and each arrives with a visible jump. In the second hundred you acquire almost no new moves at all. What changes is the grain of your attention. You begin to feel the wall thinning half a second before it does. You notice that the clay was a little wetter on Tuesday. You stop looking at your hands.

None of that shows in the pot for a long time. It is like the months a tree spends putting down root. Then one ordinary evening you throw something, set it on the board, and realise that you were thinking about the curve the whole time and not about the clay at all.

> The plateau is where the skill moves from the part of you that tries to the part of you that knows.

## How to stay

I have watched perhaps three hundred people reach this point, and the ones who get through do not seem to be the most gifted or the most stubborn. They are the ones who find something to pay attention to other than whether they are improving.

A few things that seem to help:

- **Make the same pot twenty times.** Not to perfect it. To have something constant, so that the only variable left is you.
- **Cut them in half.** A wire through the middle of a pot tells you more about your throwing than any amount of looking at the outside.
- **Keep one from each month.** Do not look at them. Then, in a year, look at them.

## The long middle

I am suspicious, now, of how much we celebrate beginning. The first step, the blank page, the courage to start. Starting is the easy part; curiosity carries you through it for free. And we celebrate mastery, at the far end, when it has become a kind of personality.

The long middle has no ceremony. It is just a person in a cold studio on a weeknight, making a cylinder that looks very much like last week's cylinder, and choosing to make another. I have come to think that this is where nearly all of the making in the world actually takes place, and that the people in it deserve more cake than anyone.
