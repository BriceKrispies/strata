The most useful drawing I ever made took about forty seconds and was of a door. A client had spent an hour describing the entrance she wanted for a small bookshop: welcoming but not twee, substantial, a door you would notice. I had nodded through all of it. Then I drew a rectangle with a handle on the left and she said, immediately, "No, it opens the other way. People come from the station."

Nothing in the previous hour had contained that fact. It was not that she had forgotten to mention it. She did not know it was a fact until she saw it wrong.

## Words agree too easily

This is the trouble with describing things. Language is accommodating. "Welcoming" and "substantial" can sit together in a sentence without the slightest friction, and two people can agree on them warmly while picturing entirely different doors. A sentence never has to decide which way the hinge goes.

A drawing does. You cannot draw a door without putting the handle somewhere. The pencil forces the question that conversation lets you skip, and that is the entire value of it. I have come to think of a sketch less as a picture of an idea than as a device for finding out where the idea is still missing.

> A drawing is the cheapest place to be wrong.

## Bad on purpose

The mistake I see most often, in students and in myself, is making the first drawing too good. A careful drawing invites careful comments: about line weight, about the typeface, about the particular blue. A rough one invites the comments you need: *that is the wrong way round, that is too big, why is that there at all.*

So I draw badly on purpose, at the start, with a thick pen that will not let me fuss. I want the drawing to look like a question. If it looks like an answer, people become polite, and politeness at that stage costs more than anything else in the project.

There is a discipline to it that took me years to learn:

- **Draw it small.** A thumbnail cannot hold detail, so it cannot hide in detail.
- **Draw three.** One drawing is a proposal. Three are a conversation about what differs between them.
- **Draw the awkward part first.** The corner, the join, the place where two rules meet. Whatever you are tempted to leave for later.

## Where the idea lives

I used to believe that I had ideas and then drew them. It is closer to the truth to say that I draw until an idea turns up. The first sketch is nearly always the obvious thing, the one anybody would have drawn. The fifth is where it starts to bend. Something on the page looks slightly wrong in an interesting way, and I follow it, and the thing I end up with is one I could not have described beforehand because I did not have it beforehand.

This is uncomfortable to admit to clients, who would like to think they are paying for vision. But I have stopped apologising for it. The work is not the transcription of a picture in my head. It is a loop between the hand and the eye in which the page keeps telling me things I did not know I thought.

## Mean it

What I ask of a drawing, in the end, is that it commit. A good sketch is specific enough to be mistaken. It says the door opens *this* way, firmly, so that someone who knows about the station can tell me I am wrong in the first forty seconds rather than the fortieth day.

The bookshop door opens to the right. It is oak, with a long brass pull worn pale in the middle. People come from the station, and it is the first thing they see.
