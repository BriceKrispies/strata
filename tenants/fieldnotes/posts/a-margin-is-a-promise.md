There is a paperback on my desk, a novel from 1974, whose inner margin is nine millimetres wide. To read the last word of each left-hand line you have to break the spine. Somebody saved perhaps twelve pages a copy by setting it that way, and fifty years later the saving is still being paid for by every reader who opens it, one cracked spine at a time.

I keep the book because it is the clearest lesson I own about what a margin is. It is not empty space. It is the part of the page that has been promised to the reader's hands, to the gutter, to the thumb, to the eye's need for somewhere to rest before it turns back. When a designer narrows it, nothing is gained from nowhere. Something is taken from someone who was not in the room.

## What whitespace is doing

People who do not design tend to see whitespace as the absence of design, which is why it is the first thing cut when the page gets crowded. The request usually arrives as a question about efficiency: *could we fit a little more above the fold?* And you can. You always can. The page does not resist. It simply becomes slightly harder to read in a way that no one will be able to point to.

Here is what the space was doing before it was removed:

1. **Grouping.** Two things close together are read as one thing. Space is the cheapest and most reliable way to say *these belong, and that does not.*
2. **Pacing.** A wide margin slows the line down. A paragraph with air above it is a new thought; one without is a continuation.
3. **Trust.** A page with room on it tells you that somebody decided what mattered and left the rest out.

None of this shows up as a feature. You cannot demonstrate a margin. You can only notice, later, that people finished the article.

## The measure

The old typographers had a word, *measure*, for the length of a line, and they were strict about it: somewhere between forty-five and seventy-five characters, sixty-six if you could manage it. The rule looks arbitrary until you watch someone read a line of a hundred and forty characters on a wide screen. Their eye reaches the end, swings back, and lands on the wrong line about one time in five. They do not know this is happening. They just feel that the text is tiring, and blame the writer.

So the margin on a wide screen is not waste. It is the difference between the width of the window and the width a human eye can track, and it belongs to the reader in the same way the paperback's gutter did.

## Generosity is a decision

What I have come to believe is that every layout encodes a view of the person on the other side of it. The crowded page thinks you are a pair of eyes to be filled. The generous page thinks you have a body, a limited attention, a thumb, somewhere else to be. It leaves room for you.

That room always costs something, and the cost is always visible to the person approving the design and invisible to the person reading it. That asymmetry is the whole difficulty. The designer's job is to stand in for the absent reader and say, about the nine millimetres: *no. That part is not ours.*
