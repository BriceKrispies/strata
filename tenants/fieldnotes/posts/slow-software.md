I have a program on my computer that I wrote in 2011. It renames photographs. It takes the date out of the file, puts it at the front of the name, and moves the file into a folder for that month. It is ninety lines long. I have not changed it in eleven years, and I use it every week.

I mention it because I have also, in that time, worked on software built by forty people, with a roadmap and a design system, that no longer exists. Not that it is unmaintained: it is *gone*, the servers off, the domain pointing at something else. Several years of careful work by good people, and the ninety lines outlived all of it.

I do not think this is an accident, and I do not think it is only about size.

## What makes a tool last

When I look at the software I have used for more than a decade, it has a few things in common, and none of them are the things we praised in planning meetings.

- **It does one job whose shape does not change.** Photographs still have dates. Text still needs searching. The need was there before the tool and will be there after.
- **Its data is legible without it.** My renamed photographs are just files in folders. If the program vanished tomorrow I would lose a convenience and nothing else.
- **It asks for nothing.** No account, no update, no connection. It runs when I run it and is otherwise inert, like a chisel in a drawer.
- **I can hold all of it in my head.** When it does something odd, I can read the whole thing in the time it takes to drink a coffee.

Put that way, the list is almost a description of a hand tool. A good plane is a hundred years old and works. Nobody has added features to it.

## The cost of the other kind

The software that did not last was better in every way you could measure in a demonstration. It was faster to start with, prettier, more capable. It also depended on eleven other services, any one of which could change its terms, and on a team that had to keep working merely for it to stay the same. It was not a thing. It was a continuous effort that looked like a thing, and when the effort stopped, so did it.

I am not against that kind of software. Most of what matters now has to be built that way; you cannot run a hospital on ninety lines. But I think we have lost the habit of asking which kind we are making, and so we build the second kind by default, even for jobs that wanted the first.

> Ask of any feature: who will be maintaining this in ten years, and will they thank me?

## A slower way to build

What I try to do now, on small things, is to build as if I will be the one to open the file again in 2037 with no memory of any of it. That changes the decisions more than I expected. I write less. I choose the boring format. I leave a note at the top saying what the thing is for and what it deliberately does not do, because the second list is the one I will need.

And I try to finish. Not to ship and iterate, but to actually reach the point where the tool does its one job and there is nothing left to add, and then to stop, and to let it sit in the drawer being correct.

It is an unfashionable ambition. A finished program generates no news. But every week I rename a few hundred photographs, and for about four seconds I am grateful to somebody who took the trouble, eleven years ago, to make a small thing properly and then leave it alone.
