For the first three years I built furniture, I thought of jigs as a kind of cheating. A jig is a thing you make in order to make the thing: a plywood sled that carries a board past a blade at exactly eleven degrees, a block with a hole in it that puts every shelf pin in the same place. Real craftsmen, I assumed, did it by eye. I had read about the Japanese joiner who could plane a shaving you could read a newspaper through, and nobody mentioned his jigs.

Then I spent a week in a shop where a man named Aldous had been making the same chair for twenty-six years. The chair has fourteen parts and nine angles, none of them ninety degrees. Aldous could not have cut one of those joints freehand to save his life, and he said so cheerfully. What he had instead was a wall of jigs, each one dark with handling, each one labelled in pencil with a date and a correction. *Seat rail, left. Shim 0.5 mm at back, the oak moved, March 2009.*

## The knowledge has to live somewhere

That wall was the chair. Not a tool for making the chair: the chair itself, in its negative form. Every decision Aldous had ever made about how the back should lean and where the arm should meet the leg was stored there, in plywood, in a form that would still be correct on a morning when he was tired or distracted or seventy.

I think about this whenever someone tells me that a good craftsperson should not need the scaffolding. The claim sounds like humility and is really a kind of vanity. It locates the skill in the hands, where it is admirable and mortal, instead of in the setup, where it is dull and durable. Skill kept in the hands has to be performed again every time. Skill built into a jig has only to be respected.

> A jig is a decision you have agreed to stop making.

## What a good jig refuses

The best ones are opinionated in a specific way. They do not make the work easy. They make one particular mistake impossible. Aldous's seat-rail jig will not let you put the rail in backwards, because years ago he put one in backwards and then glued a block where his thumb would hit it if he ever tried again.

That is the part I had wrong. I had imagined jigs as a way of avoiding difficulty, and they are closer to a way of *remembering* it. Each one is the fossil of an error. You can read a shop's history off its jigs the way you read a river's history off its banks.

A few things I look for now, when I build one:

- It should hold the work, not the worker. If I have to stand in a strange position, I will stop using it by Thursday.
- It should be obvious when it is being used wrongly. A jig that works both ways round is half a jig.
- It should be written on. Date, purpose, the correction and why.

## The same thing, elsewhere

I write software for part of my living now, and the lesson travelled with almost no translation. The test that fails when someone flips a sign, the type that will not let a draft be shown to a reader, the script that sets up a new machine without asking anything: these are jigs. They are where the knowledge goes so that it does not have to stay in somebody's head, being performed.

It is tempting to treat that work as overhead, as the cost you pay before the real work begins. Aldous would have laughed at that. He spent most of one winter remaking a single fixture, and the forty chairs he built the following year were the best he ever made. He did not think of the winter as preparation.

He thought he had been making chairs.
