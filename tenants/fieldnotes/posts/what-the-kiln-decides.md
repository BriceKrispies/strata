A wood kiln takes about four days to fire and three to cool, and for all seven of them you cannot see what you have made. You load it on a Tuesday. You brick up the door. You feed it split pine every few minutes through the night, in shifts, watching the colour of the flame at the spy hole go from orange to a white that hurts to look at. Then you seal everything and go home, and you wait most of a week to find out whether the last two months of your life were a good idea.

I have been doing this for nineteen years and the opening still frightens me. Not because the results are often bad. Because they are never what I made.

## The part that is not mine

When I put a bowl in the kiln it is a dull pink-grey thing with a chalky skin of glaze. I know its shape. I chose its shape. What I do not know is where the ash will land, which side the flame will favour, whether the glaze will run and pool in the foot or break thin and dark over the rim. A pot fired near the firebox comes out crusted and green on one shoulder, as if it had been left on a beach. The same pot two shelves back would have been pale and quiet.

So about half of every pot is authored by something that is not me. Students find this intolerable at first. They want to know how to get *that* surface, the one in the photograph, and I have to tell them that the potter who made it could not get it twice either.

> You do not finish a pot. You bring it as far as you can and then you hand it over.

## Two ways of being wrong

There is a lazy version of this idea, and I want to be clear that I do not mean it. The lazy version says: the fire decides, so my choices do not matter, so I need not make them carefully. This produces bad pots very reliably. Fire is not kind to vagueness. A wall that is slightly too thick does not come out charmingly irregular; it cracks.

The opposite mistake is the one I made for my first ten years, which was to treat the kiln as an adversary. I packed it the same way every time. I kept charts. I was trying to shrink the fire's share of the work to nothing, and what I got for it was a decade of competent, obedient, slightly dead pots.

What changed was small. I began making pots *for* a place in the kiln. This one is tall and narrow because it is going at the front, where the ash falls heaviest, and I want a long surface for it to run down. That one has a wide flat rim because flame will lie across it. I still cannot say what will happen. But I have made something that can receive what happens, instead of something that will merely survive it.

## Leaving room

I think most making has a kiln in it somewhere: a stage where the work passes out of your control and comes back altered. A writer has an editor, and then a reader. A builder has weather. Anyone who makes software has other people's hands on it within the hour.

You can respond to that by gripping harder or by caring less, and I have tried both. The third thing is harder to describe. It is to do your own part with complete precision and then to *design for the handover*, to make a thing with enough structure to hold and enough openness to be improved by what you cannot foresee.

We open the kiln on Tuesday. I do not know what is in there. I know exactly what I put in.
